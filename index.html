Course overview

How software engineering changes when AI takes part in it

Course›Overview

Generative AI, large language models and AI agents now take part in every activity of the software lifecycle, from eliciting requirements to writing, testing, reviewing and maintaining code. This course asks what that does to software engineering: which methods and tools are new, which established practices still carry the weight, and how an engineer stays accountable for software that AI helped produce. Students learn the fundamentals of software engineering first, then how modern AI tools work, then how to apply both together across the lifecycle. The goal is to systematically engineer software with AI.

Course organisation and assessment

Course organisation
Level Bachelor Computer Science, 3rd semester
Credits and format 9 KP, 4 SWS lecture + 2 SWS exercise, 14 weeks, two lectures and one exercise per week
Prerequisite One programming language, and Git. The software engineering fundamentals the course relies on (processes, requirements, design, testing, review) are taught in Block I, weeks 1 to 3.
Assessment Portfolio: semester project 50 %, weekly exercises 30 %, final presentation and reflection 20 %

Four blocks across 14 weeks, two lectures per week: Software engineering fundamentals (weeks 1 to 3), LLMs and agents (weeks 4 to 6), AI across the software engineering lifecycle (weeks 7 to 12), and Judgement and synthesis (weeks 13 and 14). The first block establishes the engineering foundation of the project without AI; the second introduces language models, agents and agentic workflows; the third walks the lifecycle phase by phase with agents as participants; the fourth asks what the engineer is accountable for. See the schedule, the weekly exercises and the semester project.

Teams of three or four choose one of two systems in week 1 and develop it throughout the semester: conventionally in Block I, with AI agents from week 4. The deliverable is a working system and documented engineering evidence.

Learning objectives

By the end of the course, students can:

  • explain the fundamentals of software engineering: processes, requirements, design, testing, review and maintenance, and why each of these activities exists;
  • explain how generative AI, large language models and AI agents work, what they can and cannot do reliably, and how they are used in software development today;
  • describe how AI changes software engineering methods and tools across the lifecycle, and select suitable AI-assisted workflows, agent frameworks and tools for a given engineering task;
  • evaluate AI-produced artifacts against appropriate criteria and credible evidence, and name what is missing before accepting them;
  • work responsibly with AI: document its use, judge the quality and security risks, and take accountability for software developed with it.

The fourth objective runs through the whole course. Most weeks end by asking it about something an AI tool just produced.

Why this course

Research on generative AI in software engineering has grown quickly and unevenly. A recent research agenda (Nguyen-Duc et al., Software: Practice and Experience, 2025) finds that generative AI can support partial automation and decision-making in all software development activities, while research so far concentrates on implementation, quality assurance and maintenance, and names dependability, accuracy and transparency as the common concerns. Surveys from industry and academia (Fan et al., ICSE-FoSE 2023) argue that the open problem is not generating code but reliably weeding out incorrect results, and that the way forward combines established software engineering techniques with language models. Vision papers for the field (Lo, ICSE-FoSE 2023) frame the goal as AI for software engineering that is trustworthy enough to delegate to and synergistic with the engineer rather than a replacement. This course is built on that view: engineering fundamentals first, AI tools second, and the two together across the lifecycle.

Refresh your knowledge

Block I teaches the fundamentals in class. If you want to read ahead, or come back to a topic later, the material below is open and free to read.

The software lifecycle and process models

SWEBOK, Guide to the Software Engineering Body of Knowledge, IEEE Computer Society. Free to download. The chapters on software engineering process, requirements, design and testing are the ones to skim.

Testing fundamentals

ISTQB Certified Tester Foundation Level, syllabus v4.0, free download. Chapters 1 and 4 cover the testing principles, test levels, and black-box and white-box techniques the lecture relies on.

Git and continuous integration

Pro Git, Chacon and Straub, second edition, free to read in full online. Chapters 1 to 3 and 6 are enough. For continuous integration, the week 1 exercise walks you through setting up a pipeline on your own repository.

Python or Java basics

The Python Tutorial, Python Software Foundation, or Learn Java, the official Java tutorials. One language, well enough to read and review code you did not write.
